# Chapter 11: Time Difference of Arrival and Hyperbolic Geometry

## 11.1 The TDOA Principle

Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) is a passive ranging technique that measures the difference in signal propagation time between two spatially separated receiving stations. Unlike active ranging methods, which require the emitter to transmit timing information, TDOA relies solely on the fact that an electromagnetic signal propagates at a known, constant speed through free space. By recording the instant at which a pulsed or continuous-wave signal arrives at each station, the system computes the difference in arrival times and converts this into a difference in path length. This difference in path length, in turn, constrains the possible locations of the emitter to a specific geometric locus.

The fundamental relationship governing TDOA is remarkably simple. If a signal emitted from a source reaches receiver one at time $t_1$ and receiver two at time $t_2$, the time difference is $\Delta t = t_1 - t_2$. Because the signal travels at the speed of light $c = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}$, the corresponding difference in range is:

$$
\Delta r = r_1 - r_2 = c \cdot \Delta t
$$

where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the distances from the emitter to receivers one and two respectively. In the Palladium Null implementation, this is computed by the function `tdoaRangeDiffM(dtSeconds)`, which returns the range difference in meters given a time difference in seconds. The inverse operation, recovering the time difference from a known range difference, is performed by `tdoaSeconds(rangeDiffM)`.

```ts
export function tdoaRangeDiffM(dtSeconds: number): number {
  return C * dtSeconds;
}
export function tdoaSeconds(rangeDiffM: number): number {
  return rangeDiffM / C;
}
```

The constant $\Delta r$ is the defining quantity of TDOA geometry. For a fixed pair of stations and a fixed measured $\Delta t$, every point in space that satisfies the condition $|p - f_1| - |p - f_2| = \Delta r$ lies on the same geometric surface. In three dimensions, this surface is a hyperboloid of revolution. When the problem is reduced to two dimensions, the surface becomes a hyperbola. The two stations serve as the foci of this hyperbola or hyperboloid. The collection of all possible emitter positions that produce the same TDOA measurement forms this curve or surface, and the intersection of multiple such curves from multiple station pairs narrows the emitter location to a single point.

This geometric principle is the mathematical foundation for passive multilateration. Multilateration differs fundamentally from triangulation. Triangulation uses angle measurements to determine position, requiring directional antennas or interferometric phase measurements at each station. Multilateration, by contrast, uses only range differences derived from time measurements. No angular information is required at any station. This makes the technique particularly attractive for civil-defense applications where simple omnidirectional receivers can be deployed across a wide area, and where the passive nature of the system means the emitter is never interrogated, never alerted, and never compromised.

The figure {{FIG:fig-tdoa-hyperbola|TDOA hyperbola family for two stations at $(\pm 10\ \text{km}, 0)$}} illustrates the family of hyperbolae generated by varying $\Delta t$ while holding the station separation fixed. Each hyperbola corresponds to a single TDOA measurement, and the family demonstrates how finer time measurements produce more tightly curved hyperbolae near the baseline perpendicular, while coarser measurements yield broader curves that intersect at shallower angles.

The practical significance of TDOA extends to numerous civil-safety domains. In the domain of public-safety radio localization, TDOA techniques are used by law enforcement and emergency services to locate transmitters operating on licensed frequencies. In the domain of aeronautical surveillance, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) has standardized related techniques in Annex 10 for passive surveillance systems. The geometry described in this chapter is applicable to all of these domains without modification, because the underlying physics is universal: any signal propagating at the speed of light produces the same hyperbolic locus for a given range difference.

## 11.2 Two-Station Hyperbola in the Plane

The two-dimensional geometry of TDOA begins with the simplest possible configuration: two receivers positioned on a baseline. Let receiver one be located at $f_1 = (-c_s, 0)$ and receiver two at $f_2 = (c_s, 0)$, where $c_s$ denotes half the baseline separation and should not be confused with the speed of light $c$, which will be clearly distinguished by context throughout this chapter. The baseline separation is therefore $2c_s$.

A point $p = (x, y)$ in the plane lies on one branch of a hyperbola if and only if the signed difference of its distances to the two foci equals a constant:

$$
|p - f_1| - |p - f_2| = \Delta r
$$

The absolute value is important: if $\Delta r$ is positive, the point is closer to receiver two and lies on the branch nearest to $f_2$. If $\Delta r$ is negative, the point is closer to receiver one and lies on the branch nearest to $f_1$. The sign of $\Delta r$ therefore determines which branch of the hyperbola the emitter resides upon. This is a critical observation for practical systems, because a single TDOA measurement without additional context cannot distinguish between the two branches without auxiliary information about which side of the baseline the emitter is expected to be on.

The standard form of the hyperbola equation is obtained by algebraic manipulation of the distance-difference condition. Squaring both sides, rearranging, and squaring again yields:

$$
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1
$$

where the semi-transverse axis $a$ is given by:

$$
a = \frac{|\Delta r|}{2}
$$

and the semi-conjugate axis $b$ is given by:

$$
b = \sqrt{c_s^2 - a^2}
$$

These relationships follow directly from the definition of a hyperbola in analytic geometry, where $a$ is the distance from the center to the vertex along the transverse axis, and $b$ determines the curvature of the asymptotes. The asymptotes themselves are the straight lines:

$$
y = \pm \frac{b}{a} x
$$

which the hyperbola approaches at large distances from the center. The asymptotes are particularly important for understanding the geometry of TDOA intersection: when two hyperbolae from different baselines intersect, the angle at which they cross determines the dilution of precision (DOP) of the solution. Intersections near the asymptotes of either hyperbola produce poor geometry, while intersections near the vertices produce well-conditioned solutions.

Several limiting behaviors are worth noting. As $\Delta r \to 0$, the semi-major axis $a \to 0$, the semi-conjugate axis $b \to c_s$, and the hyperbola approaches the perpendicular bisector of the baseline. In this limit, the TDOA measurement provides very little range information, but the locus of possible positions collapses to a line, which is equivalent to a bearing measurement in a specific sense. Conversely, as $|\Delta r| \to 2c_s$, the semi-major axis approaches the focal distance, and the hyperbola degenerates: the two branches pinch together and the curvature becomes infinite. For $|\Delta r| > 2c_s$, no real hyperbola exists, meaning that the measured time difference is physically impossible for the given station geometry. Every valid TDOA measurement must therefore satisfy $|\Delta r| < 2c_s$, or equivalently $|\Delta t| < 2c_s / c$.

The relationship between $a$, $b$, and $c_s$ is the defining characteristic of the hyperbola family for a given baseline. For a fixed baseline, each measured $\Delta t$ produces a different value of $a$ and consequently a different hyperbola. The family of all such hyperbolae, parameterized by $\Delta t$, forms the classical hyperbolic navigation lattice. Historical systems such as LORAN-C and DECCA operated on precisely this principle, using chains of transmitters to produce families of hyperbolae on which a navigator's position lay. The Palladium Null system adapts this geometry for civil-defense emitter localization, replacing the transmitters of LORAN-C with passive detection of third-party signals.

## 11.3 Worked Example 11.1 — Basic TDOA Range Difference

This worked example demonstrates the fundamental computation at the heart of every TDOA system: converting a measured time difference into a range difference. The arithmetic is straightforward but must be performed with careful attention to units, because the speed of light is a large number and the time differences involved are typically very small.

**Given:** A TDOA measurement yields $\Delta t = 12.345\ \mu\text{s} = 12.345 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}$. The two receiving stations are separated by a baseline of $20\ \text{km}$.

**Find:** The range difference $\Delta r$, the semi-transverse axis $a$, the semi-conjugate axis $b$, and a verification that the measurement is geometrically valid.

**Solution:**

The first step is to compute the range difference using the TDOA relationship:

$$
\Delta r = c \cdot \Delta t = 299\,792\,458\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \times 12.345 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}
$$

Performing the multiplication:

$$
\Delta r = 299\,792\,458 \times 12.345 \times 10^{-6}
$$

We can decompose this:

$$
299\,792\,458 \times 12 \times 10^{-6} = 3\,597.509\,496\ \text{m}
$$

$$
299\,792\,458 \times 0.345 \times 10^{-6} = 103.428\,398\,010\ \text{m}
$$

Adding these contributions:

$$
\Delta r \approx 3\,700.938\ \text{m} + 3.509\ \text{m} = 3\,703.5\ \text{m}
$$

Rounding to four significant figures: $\Delta r \approx 3\,703\ \text{m} = 3.703\ \text{km}$.

Next, we determine the semi-transverse axis $a$:

$$
a = \frac{|\Delta r|}{2} = \frac{3.703\ \text{km}}{2} = 1.8515\ \text{km}
$$

The focal distance is half the baseline:

$$
c_s = \frac{20\ \text{km}}{2} = 10\ \text{km}
$$

The semi-conjugate axis is then:

$$
b = \sqrt{c_s^2 - a^2} = \sqrt{(10)^2 - (1.8515)^2}\ \text{km}
$$

$$
b = \sqrt{100 - 3.4281}\ \text{km} = \sqrt{96.5719}\ \text{km} \approx 9.827\ \text{km}
$$

**Verification:** The measurement is valid if and only if $|\Delta r| < 2c_s$, that is, $3.703\ \text{km} < 20\ \text{km}$. This condition is satisfied, confirming that a real hyperbola exists for this TDOA measurement. The eccentricity of the hyperbola is $e = c_s / a = 10 / 1.8515 \approx 5.40$, which is well above unity as required for a hyperbola.

The asymptote slope is $b/a = 9.827 / 1.8515 \approx 5.31$, meaning the asymptotes make angles of approximately $\arctan(5.31) \approx 79.3^\circ$ with the baseline axis. This steep asymptote angle indicates that the hyperbola is relatively narrow near the vertex, which is favorable for intersection geometry when combined with a second baseline.

## 11.4 Worked Example 11.2 — Closer TDOA and Wavelength Considerations

This example examines a TDOA measurement with a much smaller time difference and explores the relationship between the range difference and the signal wavelength. The wavelength-to-range-difference ratio is a critical parameter in TDOA systems because it determines the precision with which the range difference can be estimated. A larger number of wavelengths within the range difference generally corresponds to better measurement precision, assuming the system can resolve phase or timing at the wavelength scale.

**Given:** A TDOA measurement yields $\Delta t = 1.0\ \mu\text{s} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}$. The signal frequency is variable, and we examine two cases: $f = 500\ \text{MHz}$ and $f = 100\ \text{MHz}$.

**Find:** The range difference $\Delta r$ and the ratio $\Delta r / \lambda$ for both frequencies.

**Solution:**

The range difference is computed first:

$$
\Delta r = c \cdot \Delta t = 299\,792\,458 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-6} = 299.79\ \text{m} \approx 299.8\ \text{m}
$$

Rounding: $\Delta r \approx 0.30\ \text{km}$.

For the first case, at $f = 500\ \text{MHz}$, the wavelength is computed using the relationship $\lambda = c / f$:

$$
\lambda = \frac{299\,792\,458}{500 \times 10^6} = \frac{299.792\,458}{500} = 0.5996\ \text{m} \approx 0.60\ \text{m}
$$

The number of wavelengths in the range difference is:

$$
\frac{\Delta r}{\lambda} = \frac{299.8}{0.60} \approx 500\ \text{wavelengths}
$$

This means the range difference spans approximately 500 full cycles of the electromagnetic wave at 500 MHz. A system capable of resolving even a fraction of a wavelength, such as $1/100$ of a cycle, would achieve a range-difference precision on the order of $0.60 / 100 = 6\ \text{mm}$, which is exceptionally precise.

For the second case, at $f = 100\ \text{MHz}$, the wavelength is:

$$
\lambda = \frac{299\,792\,458}{100 \times 10^6} = 2.998\ \text{m} \approx 3.0\ \text{m}
$$

The number of wavelengths in the range difference is:

$$
\frac{\Delta r}{\lambda} = \frac{299.8}{3.0} \approx 100\ \text{wavelengths}
$$

At 100 MHz, the range difference spans approximately 100 wavelengths. The precision achievable at this frequency, with the same $1/100$ cycle resolution, would be $3.0 / 100 = 30\ \text{mm}$, which is ten times coarser than at 500 MHz. This illustrates a fundamental trade-off in TDOA system design: higher frequencies provide finer range-difference resolution but may suffer from greater atmospheric absorption and reduced propagation range, while lower frequencies propagate farther but offer coarser geometric resolution.

The relationship between time measurement accuracy and range accuracy is direct and linear:

$$
\delta r = c \cdot \delta t
$$

where $\delta t$ is the timing uncertainty and $\delta r$ is the resulting range-difference uncertainty. For a timing precision of $1\ \text{ns}$, the range precision is:

$$
\delta r = 299\,792\,458 \times 10^{-9} \approx 0.30\ \text{m}
$$

This means that every nanosecond of timing accuracy translates to approximately 30 centimeters of range accuracy. Practical TDOA systems must therefore invest in precise synchronization between stations, often using GPS-disciplined oscillators or dedicated timing distribution networks, to achieve the sub-meter accuracy required for meaningful localization.

## 11.5 Three-Station Multilateration

The transition from two stations to three stations is the point at which TDOA geometry becomes practically useful for position determination. With two stations, a single TDOA measurement constrains the emitter to a hyperbola. This is a one-dimensional constraint in a two-dimensional space: the emitter could be anywhere along the curve. A second TDOA measurement from a different baseline produces a second hyperbola, and the intersection of the two hyperbolae narrows the possible emitter locations to a small number of discrete points. However, with only two baselines, ambiguity remains: there are typically two intersection points, and additional information is needed to select the correct one.

Three stations, however, produce three independent TDOA measurements (one for each pair of stations), which yield three hyperbolae. In general, three hyperbolae from three station pairs intersect at a single point, resolving all geometric ambiguities. This is the fundamental principle of three-station multilateration. It is important to emphasize that this is multilateration, not triangulation. Triangulation uses angle-of-arrival measurements at each station to determine position through intersecting bearings. Multilateration uses only range differences, which are derived purely from time measurements. No directional information is required at any station, and no angle measurement is performed.

The mathematical framework for three-station multilateration can be summarized as follows. Given three stations at known positions $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$, and measured time differences $\Delta t_{12}$, $\Delta t_{13}$, and $\Delta t_{23}$ (where $\Delta t_{ij}$ denotes the arrival time difference between station $i$ and station $j$), we compute three range differences:

$$
\Delta r_{12} = c \cdot \Delta t_{12}, \quad \Delta r_{13} = c \cdot \Delta t_{13}, \quad \Delta r_{23} = c \cdot \Delta t_{23}
$$

Each range difference defines a hyperbola with the corresponding station pair as foci. The three hyperbolae intersect at the emitter location $p$. The system of equations:

$$
|p - S_1| - |p - S_2| = \Delta r_{12}
$$
$$
|p - S_1| - |p - S_3| = \Delta r_{13}
$$
$$
|p - S_2| - |p - S_3| = \Delta r_{23}
$$

is overdetermined (three equations for two unknowns in 2D), which provides redundancy that can be exploited for error detection and correction. In practice, measurement noise means the three hyperbolae do not intersect at exactly one point, and a least-squares or maximum-likelihood estimation is used to find the best-fit emitter position.

The ICAO Annex 10, Volume III, describes the application of multilateration techniques to aeronautical surveillance, specifically in the context of secondary surveillance radar and Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) ground stations. While the ICAO standard addresses active surveillance, the geometric principles are identical to those used in passive multilateration. The open excerpts from Annex 10 provide a reference framework that confirms the universality of the hyperbolic approach to position determination. The Palladium Null system adapts these principles specifically for civil-defense emitter localization, ensuring that all outputs are labeled and framed within the appropriate safety context.

A critical property of multilateration is that it is entirely passive with respect to the emitter. The emitter does not need to transmit any identifying signal, does not need to be interrogated, and does not need to be aware of the receiving stations. The stations simply listen and record arrival times. This property is essential for civil-defense applications where the system must detect and locate emitters without alerting them, but it also means that the system can operate against any emitter within its frequency coverage area, provided the signal is strong enough to be detected and timed.

## 11.6 Worked Example 11.3 — Three-Station Solution

This worked example demonstrates the complete geometric solution for a three-station multilateration system. The stations are arranged in an equilateral triangle configuration, which is a common and well-conditioned geometry because it provides uniform baseline coverage in all directions.

**Given:** Three receiving stations at positions $S_1 = (0, 0)\ \text{km}$, $S_2 = (20, 0)\ \text{km}$, and $S_3 = (10, 17.32)\ \text{km}$. These positions form an equilateral triangle with side length $20\ \text{km}$, since the distance from $S_1$ to $S_3$ is $\sqrt{10^2 + 17.32^2} = \sqrt{100 + 299.98} \approx \sqrt{400} = 20\ \text{km}$, and similarly for $S_2$ to $S_3$. The measured time differences are $\Delta t_{12} = 12.345\ \mu\text{s}$ and $\Delta t_{13} = 6.172\ \mu\text{s}$.

**Find:** The range differences defining the three hyperbolae and a description of the intersection geometry that determines the emitter location.

**Solution:**

The first range difference is computed from $\Delta t_{12}$:

$$
\Delta r_{12} = c \cdot \Delta t_{12} = 299\,792\,458 \times 12.345 \times 10^{-6}
$$

Following the same arithmetic as in Example 11.1:

$$
\Delta r_{12} \approx 3\,703\ \text{m} = 3.703\ \text{km}
$$

This defines a hyperbola with foci at $S_1$ and $S_2$. The semi-transverse axis for this hyperbola is $a_{12} = 3.703 / 2 = 1.8515\ \text{km}$, and the focal distance is $c_{s,12} = 20 / 2 = 10\ \text{km}$. The semi-conjugate axis is $b_{12} = \sqrt{100 - 3.428} \approx 9.827\ \text{km}$.

The second range difference is computed from $\Delta t_{13}$:

$$
\Delta r_{13} = c \cdot \Delta t_{13} = 299\,792\,458 \times 6.172 \times 10^{-6}
$$

Performing the multiplication:

$$
\Delta r_{13} = 299\,792\,458 \times 6.172 \times 10^{-6} \approx 1\,850.0\ \text{m} = 1.850\ \text{km}
$$

This defines a hyperbola with foci at $S_1$ and $S_3$. The semi-transverse axis is $a_{13} = 1.850 / 2 = 0.925\ \text{km}$, and the focal distance is $c_{s,13} = 20\ \text{km}$ (the equilateral triangle has all sides equal to 20 km). The semi-conjugate axis is $b_{13} = \sqrt{400 - 0.8556} \approx 19.98\ \text{km}$.

The third range difference, $\Delta r_{23}$, can be derived from the other two. Since $\Delta r_{12} = |p - S_1| - |p - S_2|$ and $\Delta r_{13} = |p - S_1| - |p - S_3|$, the difference gives:

$$
\Delta r_{23} = \Delta r_{12} - \Delta r_{13} = 3.703 - 1.850 = 1.853\ \text{km}
$$

This third hyperbola has foci at $S_2$ and $S_3$.

**Intersection geometry:** The three hyperbolae, defined by the three range differences, intersect at the emitter location. The first hyperbola (foci $S_1$, $S_2$) is relatively narrow, with a semi-transverse axis of 1.85 km, meaning it curves sharply near the baseline. The second hyperbola (foci $S_1$, $S_3$) is also narrow but oriented at 60 degrees to the first, since the baseline $S_1 S_3$ is rotated 60 degrees from the baseline $S_1 S_2$. The third hyperbola (foci $S_2$, $S_3$) is similarly oriented. The intersection of these three curves provides the emitter coordinates $(x, y)$.

In this specific example, the emitter is located at a point where the range differences are consistent with all three measured time differences. The exact coordinates require numerical solution of the nonlinear system of hyperbola equations, but the geometric principle is clear: the three-station configuration eliminates the two-fold ambiguity that persists with two stations, because the third hyperbola intersects the two existing hyperbolae at a single point. This is the geometric basis for passive emitter localization, and it scales naturally to more stations, where additional baselines provide further redundancy and improved accuracy.

## 11.7 Hyperboloid in 3D

The extension from two-dimensional hyperbolic geometry to three-dimensional hyperboloidal geometry is a natural one. In three dimensions, the locus of points with a constant range difference to two fixed foci is not a hyperbola but a hyperboloid of revolution. Specifically, the surface defined by $|p - f_1| - |p - f_2| = \Delta r$ is a two-sheeted hyperboloid, with each sheet corresponding to one sign of $\Delta r$. The axis of revolution is the line connecting the two foci, and the cross-section perpendicular to this axis at any point is a circle.

The equation of the hyperboloid of revolution in Cartesian coordinates, with foci at $(0, 0, \pm c_s)$, is:

$$
\frac{x^2 + y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{a^2} = -1
$$

where $a = |\Delta r|/2$ and $b = \sqrt{c_s^2 - a^2}$, exactly as in the two-dimensional case. The minus sign in the equation reflects the fact that the axis of the hyperboloid is the $z$-axis (the line through the foci), rather than the transverse axis being aligned with the $x$-axis as in the two-dimensional hyperbola.

In a practical three-dimensional TDOA system, each measured time difference produces a hyperboloid of revolution. The intersection of two hyperboloids is generally a space curve, not a single point. Specifically, two hyperboloids from two different baselines intersect along a curve that lies on both surfaces. This curve can be quite complex in general, but for stations arranged in a convenient geometry, the intersection simplifies considerably. The intersection curve provides a one-dimensional constraint on the emitter position, reducing the three-dimensional problem to a one-dimensional search along the curve.

Three TDOA measurements produce three hyperboloids, and the intersection of three hyperboloids is generically a discrete set of points. In the absence of measurement errors and with a well-conditioned station geometry, this set contains exactly one point, which is the emitter location. The three hyperboloids intersect at a point that satisfies all three range-difference equations simultaneously. This enables full three-dimensional passive localization, providing range, azimuth, and elevation information about the emitter.

The practical implications of three-dimensional TDOA localization are significant for civil-defense applications. A system that can determine the three-dimensional position of an emitter can assess the threat level more accurately than a system that provides only a two-dimensional bearing or range. For example, knowing the elevation of a transmitter allows the system to determine whether it is on the ground, on a rooftop, or at altitude, which in turn affects the assessment of potential impact. The three-dimensional solution also allows the system to compute the emitter's altitude above sea level, which is essential for coordinating with emergency-response authorities.

The relationship between the number of stations and the dimensionality of the localization problem follows a general rule. In $n$-dimensional space, $n$ independent range-difference measurements are required to determine the emitter position, assuming the stations are in general position (no degenerate configurations). In two dimensions, two baselines (three stations) are needed for a unique solution, with a third baseline providing redundancy. In three dimensions, three baselines (four stations) are needed for a unique solution, with additional baselines providing improved accuracy and robustness against measurement errors.

The mathematical properties of hyperboloid intersections are well understood and provide the theoretical foundation for the numerical algorithms used in practical TDOA solvers. When two hyperboloids intersect, their intersection curve can be characterized analytically in certain symmetric configurations. For example, when the two baselines are perpendicular and of equal length, the intersection curve reduces to a circle or an ellipse, depending on the range differences. In the general case, however, the intersection curve is a space curve of degree four, and its computation requires iterative numerical methods such as Newton-Raphson or gradient descent. These methods are well-conditioned when the station geometry is non-degenerate, meaning that no three stations are collinear and the baselines subtend a significant angle from the emitter position. The condition number of the intersection problem depends on the geometric dilution of precision, which is a function of the emitter's position relative to the station constellation. Emitters located far from the station centroid experience higher dilution of precision, while emitters near the centroid experience lower dilution and therefore more accurate position estimates. This relationship between station geometry and position accuracy is a central concern in TDOA system design and is analyzed in detail in the context of civil-defense localization networks.

The three-dimensional TDOA problem can also be formulated as a system of nonlinear equations that are linearized through a Taylor series expansion around an initial estimate. This linearization yields a set of linear equations that can be solved using least-squares methods, and the process is iterated until convergence. The Jacobian of the system relates small changes in the emitter position to small changes in the range differences, and its singular values determine the sensitivity of the solution to measurement noise. A well-conditioned Jacobian has singular values that are well separated from zero, indicating that the solution is robust to noise. A poorly conditioned Jacobian has small singular values, indicating that the solution is sensitive to even minor measurement errors. The station geometry directly affects the singular values of the Jacobian, which is why careful placement of receiving stations is essential for achieving accurate and reliable localization.

## 11.8 Dual-Use Framing

The geometry and computational methods described in this chapter serve exclusively civil-defense emitter localization and shelter alerting purposes. The TDOA principles, hyperbolic geometry, and multilateration techniques presented here are applied within a framework that is strictly oriented toward public safety. No covert collection, no targeting, no weapon guidance, and no military application is intended or implied by the mathematics described above. The geometry of hyperbolae and hyperboloids is a neutral mathematical tool; its application depends entirely on the purpose of the system that employs it. In the Palladium Null project, that purpose is civil defense.

All outputs from the system are labeled SANDBOX CIVIL DEFENSE ONLY. This labeling convention ensures that every result produced by the TDOA localization module is unambiguously associated with the civil-defense context and not misinterpreted as serving any other purpose. The labeling is applied at every level of the processing chain, from the raw time-difference measurements through the hyperbolic fitting, the multilateration solution, and the final position estimate. This ensures traceability and accountability at every stage.

The reference standards cited in this chapter are drawn exclusively from open, publicly available sources. ICAO Annex 10, Volume III, describes surveillance and collision avoidance systems and is available as an open standard excerpt. The references by Blance et al. and Curtiss et al. are published works that describe passive location techniques and passive radar systems from an educational and engineering perspective. These references are cited for their technical content, not for any operational guidance, and all excerpts used are limited to publicly available material.

The dual-use nature of TDOA technology is acknowledged, but the framing in this chapter is unambiguous. The mathematics of hyperbolic geometry does not change based on the application. A hyperbola is a hyperbola regardless of whether it is used to locate a lost hiker, a downed aircraft, or a transmitter of interest. The distinction lies in the intent of the operator, the governance of the system, and the labeling of the outputs. In the Palladium Null framework, all three of these are oriented toward civil defense and public safety.
