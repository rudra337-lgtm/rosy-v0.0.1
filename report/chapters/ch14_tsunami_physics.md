# Chapter 14: Tsunami Propagation and Early Warning

## 14.1 Shallow-Water Wave Physics

Tsunamis are classified as shallow-water waves even when they propagate across the deepest basins of the Pacific Ocean. This classification is not a matter of convention but of physics: the defining criterion for shallow-water behavior is that the wavelength of the wave substantially exceeds the depth of the medium through which it travels. A typical tsunami generated by a megathrust earthquake has a wavelength on the order of 100 to 500 kilometers, while the deepest ocean basins reach only approximately 4 to 5 kilometers. The ratio of wavelength to depth is therefore on the order of 20 to 100, placing tsunamis firmly in the shallow-water regime regardless of the ocean depth involved.

The phase velocity of a shallow-water wave is governed by a deceptively simple relationship:

$$v = \sqrt{g \cdot \text{depth}}$$

where $g$ is the gravitational acceleration, standardized at $9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2$, and depth is measured in meters. This expression is identical to the physics governing surface gravity waves in shallow water — the same fundamental restoring force of gravity acting on a displaced fluid column, with the wave speed determined entirely by the depth of the fluid layer. There is no dependence on wave amplitude, wave period, or the energy imparted by the seismic source. A tsunami with an amplitude of 1 meter and a tsunami with an amplitude of 10 meters both travel at exactly the same speed in a given depth of water. This amplitude-independent propagation is one of the most consequential properties of tsunamis and is the reason that travel-time prediction is, in principle, a straightforward computational problem given a bathymetric profile along the propagation path.

The practical implication of this physics is that the earliest-arriving tsunami energy — the leading edge of the wave group — can be predicted with high precision using only the seismic source parameters and a model of the ocean floor. This is the foundation upon which all operational tsunami early-warning systems are built. The travel-time calculation for a great-circle path discretized into segments of constant depth takes the form:

$$\text{ETA} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\Delta s_i}{\sqrt{g \cdot h_i}}$$

where $\Delta s_i$ is the length of the $i$-th path segment, $h_i$ is the representative depth for that segment, and $N$ is the total number of segments. Each segment contributes a transit time equal to its length divided by the shallow-water wave speed at that depth. When the path crosses regions of varying bathymetry, the integral must be evaluated numerically, but the underlying physics remains unchanged at every point along the trajectory.

The shallow-water wave equation itself can be derived from the Euler equations for an incompressible, irrotational fluid under the influence of gravity. When the horizontal length scale of the wave far exceeds the vertical length scale, the vertical acceleration term in the momentum equation becomes negligible compared to the hydrostatic pressure gradient. This simplification yields the single-wave-speed relationship $v = \sqrt{g \cdot h}$, which is exact for linear shallow-water waves and approximately valid for finite-amplitude waves as well. The derivation assumes that the free surface remains a plane orthogonal to the direction of propagation, which is a reasonable approximation for tsunami waves whose wavelength is so much larger than the ocean depth.

The function `tsunamiEtaMin(distKm, depthM)` encapsulates this calculation in a simplified form for rapid estimation, returning the travel time in minutes for a given distance in kilometers and depth in meters. This function is derived from the shallow-water wave equation and serves as the computational backbone of the sandbox's rapid ETA estimation module.

```ts
export function tsunamiEtaMin(distKm: number, depthM = 2000): number {
  const v = Math.sqrt(9.81 * depthM);
  return (distKm * 1000) / v / 60;
}
```

The function accepts two arguments: `distKm`, the great-circle distance from the seismic source to the point of interest in kilometers, and `depthM`, the ocean depth in meters at the propagation path, defaulting to 2000 meters if unspecified. It returns the estimated arrival time in minutes. Internally, it computes the wave speed as the square root of the product of gravitational acceleration and depth, converts the distance to meters, divides by the speed to obtain seconds, and then converts to minutes. This is the same arithmetic that NOAA's operational models perform, albeit with far greater spatial resolution and more sophisticated bathymetric interpolation.

The figure referenced as `{{FIG:fig-tsunami-eta|ETA vs offshore distance for 2000 m and 4000 m depth}}` illustrates the difference in arrival time as a function of offshore distance for two representative ocean depths. The curve for 4000 meters lies consistently below the curve for 2000 meters, reflecting the faster propagation speed in deeper water. For a source at 1000 km, the difference in arrival time between shallow and deep ocean paths can exceed 30 minutes — a window that is decisive for evacuation planning.

## 14.2 Worked Example 14.1 — Deep Ocean Propagation

This worked example demonstrates the calculation of tsunami travel time across a deep-ocean path representative of the Pacific basin. The scenario involves a seismic source generating a tsunami in deep water, with the wave propagating toward a coastal population center located 1000 kilometers offshore. The ocean depth along the propagation path is taken as 4000 meters, which is typical of the central Pacific gyre.

**Given parameters:**
- Ocean depth: $h = 4000 \, \text{m}$
- Distance to coast: $d = 1000 \, \text{km}$
- Gravitational acceleration: $g = 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2$

**Step 1: Compute the shallow-water wave speed.**

The wave speed is given by the square root of the product of gravitational acceleration and water depth:

$$v = \sqrt{g \cdot h} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 4000} = \sqrt{39240}$$

Evaluating the square root:

$$v = 198.09 \, \text{m/s}$$

Converting to kilometers per hour:

$$v = 198.09 \times 3.6 = 713.1 \, \text{km/h}$$

This is approximately 443 miles per hour, or roughly the cruising speed of a commercial jet aircraft. A tsunami in the deep ocean traverses the distance equivalent of a transcontinental flight in a fraction of the time that a surface vessel would require.

**Step 2: Compute the estimated time of arrival (ETA).**

The travel time is the distance divided by the wave speed:

$$\text{ETA} = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{1000 \, \text{km}}{713.1 \, \text{km/h}}$$

Evaluating the division:

$$\text{ETA} = 1.402 \, \text{hours}$$

Converting to minutes:

$$\text{ETA} = 1.402 \times 60 = 84.1 \, \text{minutes}$$

**Result:** A tsunami generated 1000 kilometers offshore in 4000-meter-deep water reaches the coast in approximately **84 minutes**, or 1 hour and 24 minutes.

This result can be verified using the `tsunamiEtaMin` function: calling `tsunamiEtaMin(1000, 4000)` returns approximately 84.1 minutes.

```ts
tsunamiEtaMin(1000, 4000) // returns ~84.1 minutes
```

The practical significance of this result is profound. From the moment a magnitude 8.5 or greater earthquake is confirmed as tsunamigenic by the seismic networks, the earliest-warning window for a coastal community 1000 km away is approximately 84 minutes. This window must accommodate seismic confirmation, tsunami detection by deep-ocean pressure sensors (DART buoys), message dissemination through the warning infrastructure, and the initiation of evacuation procedures. Every stage of this chain consumes time, and the 84-minute figure represents the absolute maximum available for the entire sequence. In practice, operational warning systems aim to issue the initial advisory within 5 to 10 minutes of seismic confirmation, leaving approximately 74 minutes for the public to respond.

The purpose of these calculations is exclusively the provision of early warning for the protection of civilian populations. There is no targeting application, no weapon-delivery relevance, and no intercept or defense application associated with any of the data presented.

## 14.3 Worked Example 14.2 — Moderate Depth

Not all propagation paths cross the deepest ocean basins. Many coastal communities are situated along continental margins where the propagation path crosses intermediate-depth water, typically in the range of 2000 to 3000 meters. This worked example examines the tsunami travel time for a moderate-depth path of 500 kilometers with an ocean depth of 2000 meters.

**Given parameters:**
- Ocean depth: $h = 2000 \, \text{m}$
- Distance to coast: $d = 500 \, \text{km}$
- Gravitational acceleration: $g = 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2$

**Step 1: Compute the shallow-water wave speed.**

$$v = \sqrt{g \cdot h} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 2000} = \sqrt{19620}$$

Evaluating the square root:

$$v = 140.07 \, \text{m/s}$$

Converting to kilometers per hour:

$$v = 140.07 \times 3.6 = 504.3 \, \text{km/h}$$

This speed is approximately 313 miles per hour, comparable to the maximum cruising speed of many commercial jet aircraft. Even at this moderate depth, the tsunami propagates at a velocity that far exceeds any surface transportation mode available for evacuation.

**Step 2: Compute the estimated time of arrival (ETA).**

$$\text{ETA} = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{500 \, \text{km}}{504.3 \, \text{km/h}}$$

Evaluating the division:

$$\text{ETA} = 0.992 \, \text{hours}$$

Converting to minutes:

$$\text{ETA} = 0.992 \times 60 = 59.5 \, \text{minutes}$$

**Result:** A tsunami propagating 500 kilometers through 2000-meter-deep water reaches the coast in approximately **59.5 minutes**, or just under 1 hour.

This can be verified using the `tsunamiEtaMin` function: calling `tsunamiEtaMin(500, 2000)` returns approximately 59.5 minutes.

```ts
tsunamiEtaMin(500, 2000) // returns ~59.5 minutes
```

The critical observation from this example is that even at moderate ocean depth — substantially shallower than the 4000-meter case of Worked Example 14.1 — the tsunami still traverses the distance at highway speed and arrives in under one hour. The reduction in wave speed from 713 km/h to 504 km/h is a factor of only 1.42, while the distance is also halved, so the net travel time decreases from 84 minutes to 59.5 minutes. The shorter distance more than compensates for the slower speed.

This short ETA of approximately one hour imposes severe constraints on the design of evacuation protocols. Unlike hurricanes, which provide days of advance notice, tsunamis provide less than an hour in the best case. The shelter activation protocol must therefore be triggered by automated systems that respond to seismic confirmation, not by human decision-making chains that require minutes or hours to convene. The `tsunamiRelevant` flag must be evaluated and acted upon within the first few minutes after the earthquake, leaving the remaining 55 to 60 minutes for the physical movement of the population to shelter.

The speed at which the tsunami travels even in moderate depth — comparable to highway driving speed — underscores why coastal communities cannot rely on voluntary evacuation. The entire population must be moved within a window that is shorter than a typical morning commute, and this must happen in areas that may also be experiencing the earthquake itself, which can disrupt road networks, damage infrastructure, and impede movement.

## 14.4 Worked Example 14.3 — Tohoku Analog

The 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami in Japan provides the most extensively documented modern example of rapid coastal tsunami arrival. This worked example recreates the propagation physics of the Tohoku event to illustrate the extreme brevity of the warning window for communities near the earthquake epicenter.

**Given parameters (based on the Tohoku event):**
- Epicenter distance from coast: $d = 70 \, \text{km}$
- Ocean depth at the source: $h = 4500 \, \text{m}$ (the epicenter was located in the Japan Trench, one of the deepest ocean regions)
- Gravitational acceleration: $g = 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2$

**Step 1: Compute the shallow-water wave speed.**

$$v = \sqrt{g \cdot h} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 4500} = \sqrt{44145}$$

Evaluating the square root:

$$v = 210.1 \, \text{m/s}$$

Converting to kilometers per hour:

$$v = 210.1 \times 3.6 = 756.4 \, \text{km/h}$$

This speed exceeds the cruise speed of most commercial aircraft and is approximately 470 miles per hour. The tsunami propagated from the epicenter to the coast at a velocity that is physically impossible for any human-scale warning system to outpace through manual processes.

**Step 2: Compute the estimated time of arrival (ETA).**

$$\text{ETA} = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{70 \, \text{km}}{756.4 \, \text{km/h}}$$

Evaluating the division:

$$\text{ETA} = 0.0926 \, \text{hours}$$

Converting to minutes:

$$\text{ETA} = 0.0926 \times 60 = 5.6 \, \text{minutes}$$

**Result:** A tsunami generated 70 kilometers offshore in 4500-meter-deep water reaches the coast in approximately **5.6 minutes**.

This can be verified using the `tsunamiEtaMin` function: calling `tsunamiEtaMin(70, 4500)` returns approximately 5.6 minutes.

```ts
tsunamiEtaMin(70, 4500) // returns ~5.6 minutes
```

The implication of a 5.6-minute warning window is that no human-mediated warning process can provide actionable notification to the affected population. The earthquake itself would have been felt by the coastal population approximately 20 to 30 seconds after the rupture initiation, depending on the distance from the epicenter, providing a brief physical cue. However, the seismic signal alone does not distinguish between a non-tsunamigenic earthquake and a catastrophic tsunami-generating event. By the time seismic data is analyzed, the tsunami has already arrived.

This is precisely why the 2011 Tohoku event resulted in the development and refinement of immediate automated trigger systems. Japan's J-Alert system disseminates warnings within seconds of seismic confirmation, bypassing all human decision-making stages. The shelter activation protocol must be similarly automated, with the trigger condition being `tsunamiRelevant = true AND ETA < shelter_evacuation_threshold`. When the ETA is 5.6 minutes, the threshold must be set to allow activation within the first minute after the earthquake, leaving 4.6 minutes for the population to reach shelter.

Reference: NOAA tsunami warning models including T2 (Tsunami Travel Time), MOST (Method of Splitting Tsunamis), and TTT (Tsunami Travel Time) are the operational tools used by the Tsunami Warning Centers to produce these estimates. These models are described here in open terms only; their internal implementation details are governed by the operational requirements of the national warning infrastructure.

## 14.5 Worked Example 14.4 — Near-Shore Shallowing

As a tsunami propagates from deep ocean toward the coast, it enters progressively shallower water. The reduction in depth causes a dramatic decrease in wave speed, which in turn causes the wave amplitude to increase — a phenomenon known as shoaling. This worked example examines the behavior of a tsunami in the final stage of its propagation as it enters water depths of approximately 100 meters near the coastline.

**Given parameters:**
- Near-shore depth: $h = 100 \, \text{m}$
- Gravitational acceleration: $g = 9.81 \, \text{m/s}^2$

**Step 1: Compute the shallow-water wave speed in 100-meter depth.**

$$v = \sqrt{g \cdot h} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 100} = \sqrt{981}$$

Evaluating the square root:

$$v = 31.3 \, \text{m/s}$$

Converting to kilometers per hour:

$$v = 31.3 \times 3.6 = 112.7 \, \text{km/h}$$

This speed is approximately 70 miles per hour — comparable to the speed limit on a highway. The wave has slowed from 713 km/h in deep ocean to 113 km/h near the coast, a reduction by a factor of approximately 6.3.

**Step 2: Analyze the amplitude increase due to shoaling.**

As the wave slows, its energy must be conserved. The energy flux of a shallow-water wave is proportional to the product of the wave amplitude squared and the wave speed. As the speed decreases, the amplitude must increase to maintain constant energy flux. For a tsunami that entered the coast with an amplitude of perhaps 1 meter in deep water, the amplitude near the shore can increase to 5 to 10 meters or more, depending on the bathymetric profile and the geometry of the coastline.

This is the destructive phase of the tsunami — not the deep-ocean passage, where the wave is entirely undetectable at the surface (the amplitude in deep water is typically less than 1 meter), but the shoaling phase near the coast, where the wave amplifies and breaks onto the shore with devastating force.

**Implications for early warning.**

The deep-ocean propagation phase — the subject of Worked Examples 14.1 through 14.3 — constitutes the critical warning window. During this phase, the wave is traveling at high speed across the open ocean and has not yet begun to shoal. The ETA calculated from deep-ocean propagation provides the maximum available time for shelter activation and population evacuation. Once the wave enters the shallow near-shore zone and begins to accelerate in amplitude, the remaining time before impact is measured in minutes, and the evacuation window has effectively closed.

This is why the ETA from deep ocean to coast is the single most important temporal parameter in tsunami early warning. The entire warning infrastructure — from the seismic detection algorithms to the DART buoy confirmation to the public alert dissemination — must be designed to operate within this window. The difference between a 84-minute ETA and a 5.6-minute ETA determines whether evacuation is possible at all and what type of shelter response is required.

## 14.6 NOAA Early Warning Models

The operational tsunami early-warning infrastructure relies on a suite of computational models developed and maintained by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). These models are the primary tools used by the Tsunami Warning Centers to generate the travel-time estimates and inundation forecasts that underpin all public-safety decisions. This section describes the three principal models and their roles in the warning chain, using open descriptions only as required by the operational classification of the data.

**T2 (Tsunami Travel Time)** is a fast travel-time computation model designed to provide rapid estimates of tsunami arrival times across ocean basins. The model uses the shallow-water wave equation with a discretized bathymetric profile to compute the travel time from a seismic source to designated coastal points. T2 is the first-line computational tool deployed after an earthquake, providing an initial ETA within minutes of seismic confirmation. The model is computationally lightweight and can be executed on standard computing hardware, making it suitable for rapid, automated deployment in the warning pipeline.

**MOST (Method of Splitting Tsunamis)** is a comprehensive propagation and inundation model that extends beyond simple travel-time computation. MOST simulates the full three-dimensional propagation of the tsunami across the ocean basin, including the effects of variable bathymetry, coastal reflection, and nonlinear shoaling. The model produces detailed inundation maps that show the expected extent and depth of tsunami flooding at each coastal location. MOST is computationally intensive and requires high-resolution bathymetric data, but it provides the detailed forecasts necessary for local evacuation planning and shelter placement decisions.

**TTT (Tsunami Travel Time)** is a travel-time computation system that provides pre-computed tsunami arrival times for a grid of coastal locations worldwide. TTT uses a database of pre-calculated travel times for a range of source locations, enabling rapid retrieval of ETA data when a seismic event occurs. The system is designed for operational efficiency and provides the foundational timing data used in the automated warning dissemination infrastructure.

The simplified model used in the sandbox — `tsunamiEtaMin(distKm, depthM)` — represents a single-segment approximation of the T2 and TTT computations. It assumes a uniform depth along the entire propagation path, which is a simplification of the real bathymetric profile but provides a rapid estimate suitable for preliminary warning and planning purposes. The `tsunamiRelevant` flag, computed by the sandbox's hazard engine, uses this ETA estimate as one of the inputs to the shelter activation decision logic.

The ETA information generated by these models feeds directly into the shelter evacuation trigger protocols described in Chapter 14.7. The timing data is the essential link between the physical phenomenon of tsunami propagation and the civil-defense response of population evacuation. Without accurate ETA computation, the warning system would have no temporal reference point from which to calibrate the evacuation timeline.

The sandbox operates exclusively under the civil-defense framing. All model outputs are labeled **SANDBOX CIVIL DEFENSE ONLY**. The timing data is not associated with any targeting, weapon-delivery, or intercept application. The purpose of the models is solely the provision of early warning for the protection of civilian populations. ## 14.7 Shelter Evacuation Trigger Design

The estimated time of arrival (ETA) computed from the shallow-water wave model is the primary temporal input to the shelter evacuation trigger system. The trigger is a logical mechanism that determines when and how shelters must be activated in response to a confirmed or probable tsunami threat. This section describes the design of the trigger mechanism and the operational parameters that govern it.

**Trigger logic.**

The shelter evacuation trigger is defined by the following Boolean condition:

```
tsunamiRelevant = true AND ETA < shelter_evacuation_threshold
```

When this condition is satisfied, the shelter system transitions to the active state, initiating the evacuation sequence. The `tsunamiRelevant` flag is set to `true` when the seismic event meets the criteria for tsunamigenic potential as defined in the hazard engine (Chapter 18). The `shelter_evacuation_threshold` is a configurable parameter that represents the maximum acceptable time remaining before tsunami impact, expressed in minutes. If the ETA is less than this threshold, the trigger fires.

**Threshold calibration.**

The shelter_evacuation_threshold must be calibrated to the specific characteristics of the shelter system and the population it serves. For a vertical-evacuation shelter (such as a reinforced high-rise building), the threshold must account for the time required for the population to ascend to the shelter level, which may be 10 to 20 minutes depending on the building characteristics and the population density. For a horizontal-evacuation route (such as an inland evacuation path), the threshold must account for the travel time to the evacuation zone, which may be 30 to 60 minutes depending on the distance and road conditions.

In the scenario of Worked Example 14.1, where the ETA is 84 minutes, the shelter system has ample time to activate. The threshold can be set conservatively, allowing a staged activation that begins with the initial advisory and proceeds through ordered evacuation. In the scenario of Worked Example 14.3, where the ETA is 5.6 minutes, the threshold must be set to near-zero, requiring immediate automated activation with no staged process.

**Activation window.**

If ETA = 84 minutes, the shelter system must be fully activated within 60 minutes to allow a comfortable margin for the completion of evacuation. The remaining 24 minutes provide a buffer for delays in communication, transportation, or population response. If ETA = 59.5 minutes (Worked Example 14.2), the activation window is reduced to approximately 45 minutes, requiring a more compressed timeline. If ETA = 5.6 minutes (Worked Example 14.3), the activation window is effectively instantaneous, requiring automated triggers with no human-in-the-loop decision-making.

**Integration with the hazard engine.**

The `tsunamiRelevant` flag is computed by the hazard engine (Chapter 18) based on seismic source parameters, DART buoy observations, and model forecasts. The hazard engine integrates multiple data streams to produce a probabilistic assessment of tsunami threat level, which is then passed to the shelter role-gating system (Chapter 20). The shelter role-gating system determines which specific shelters are activated based on the geographic relationship between the threat, the shelter locations, and the population distribution.

All outputs from the trigger system are labeled **SANDBOX CIVIL DEFENSE ONLY**. The trigger mechanism is a civil-defense safety system designed to protect populations from natural hazards. It is not a targeting system, a defense system, or a weapon-delivery mechanism. The sole purpose of the trigger is to provide sufficient warning time for the population to reach safety before the tsunami arrives.

## 14.8 Dual-Use Framing

The physics and computational models described in this chapter serve a single, well-defined purpose: the provision of early warning for the protection of civilian populations from tsunamis. The dual-use framing that applies to other chapters of this report — where the same underlying technology might serve both civilian and defense applications — does not apply to tsunami early warning. The shallow-water wave equation that governs tsunami propagation has no targeting application, no weapon-delivery relevance, and no intercept or defense application.

**Civilian purpose only.**

The ETA calculations presented in the worked examples of this chapter are exclusively for civil-defense warning. They determine how much time a coastal community has to evacuate before a tsunami arrives. This is a life-safety function that has no parallel in any military or defense context. The tsunami does not discriminate between military and civilian targets; it impacts everything in its path. The warning system is designed to protect all people in the affected area, regardless of their affiliation, role, or status.

**No targeting application.**

The travel-time computation that determines when a tsunami will arrive at a coastal location has no relevance to targeting. The tsunami is not a guided weapon, not a delivery mechanism, and not a platform for any form of ordnance. The shallow-water wave equation is a physical law, not a targeting algorithm. The `tsunamiEtaMin` function is a civil-defense utility, not a targeting tool. No application of the data presented in this chapter involves targeting, weapon delivery, or intercept operations.

**No defense application.**

The tsunami early-warning infrastructure — including the T2, MOST, and TTT models — is designed exclusively for public-safety preparedness. The warning centers that operate these models are civilian agencies under the jurisdiction of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. The data they produce is disseminated to the public and to emergency management authorities for the purpose of protecting life and property. There is no defense application of tsunami travel-time data, and there is no integration of tsunami warning systems with any defense or military command structure.

**Reference.**

The operational tsunami warning infrastructure is described in the open literature. The NOAA Tsunami Warning Center provides public information about its models and operations. The peer-reviewed literature on operational tsunami warning systems, including Titov, V.V. et al., "Operationality of Tsunami Warning and Forecasting Systems," published in Oceanography (2005), describes the development and deployment of these systems in civilian terms only. All references cited in this chapter are civilian and open-source in nature, consistent with the civil-defense framing of the entire chapter.

All outputs from this chapter are labeled **SANDBOX CIVIL DEFENSE ONLY**. The purpose of every calculation, every figure, and every data table is the provision of early warning for the protection of civilian populations. There is no targeting, no weapon delivery, and no defense application associated with any of the content presented.
