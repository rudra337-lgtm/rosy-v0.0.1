# Chapter 9: Signal-to-Noise Ratio Literacy

## 9.1 The Friis Receive Equation

The Friis receive equation forms the foundational relationship for all passive illumination scenarios analyzed in this work. When an external radio-frequency source—an FM broadcast tower, a digital television transmitter, or a satellite downlink—serves as an illuminator of opportunity, the receive-only system captures a fraction of the radiated power. The equation expresses the received power in decibel terms, which is the standard convention for link-budget analysis across the telecommunications and radar engineering disciplines.

For a passive illuminator scenario, the received power $P_r$ is given by:

$$P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - \text{FSPL} \quad \text{[dB]}$$

Each term in this expression carries a specific physical meaning. $P_t$ represents the transmit power of the illuminator, expressed in dBW (decibels relative to one watt). In the context of civil-defense illumination, $P_t$ is typically in the kilowatt class for FM broadcast stations (50 kW) or digital television transmitters (up to 100 kW or more). The term $G_t$ is the transmit antenna gain measured in dBi (decibels relative to an isotropic radiator), and $G_r$ is the receive antenna gain, also in dBi. The free-space path loss, FSPL, accounts for the geometric spreading of the electromagnetic wavefront as it propagates through free space from the illuminator to the receive point.

The FSPL term is derived directly from the inverse-square law and is computed as:

$$\text{FSPL} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi R}{\lambda}\right) \quad \text{[dB]}$$

where $R$ is the range from the illuminator to the receiver in meters, and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the illuminator signal in meters, related to frequency $f$ by $\lambda = c / f$ with $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s being the exact speed of light in vacuum. This expression originates from Chapter 7 of this work, where the electromagnetic fundamentals of free-space propagation were established. The $4\pi$ factor arises from the fact that the transmitted power spreads uniformly over the surface of a sphere of radius $R$, and the effective capture area of an isotropic receive antenna is $\lambda^2 / (4\pi)$.

It is critical to emphasize that no transmitter design parameters are used in this analysis. The framework is strictly receive-only: we compute what power arrives at a given location from a known or assumed illuminator. There is no consideration of transmitter feedback, antenna matching networks, amplifier design, or any other transmit-side engineering concern. The illuminator is treated as a black box characterized solely by its output power, frequency, and antenna gain pattern. This receive-only philosophy is central to the civil-defense application domain, where the objective is to assess whether a given location receives sufficient signal strength from existing broadcast infrastructure to support reception of emergency information.

The implementation of this equation in the Palladium Null codebase is found in the function `illuminatorSnrDb` in `src/physics/index.ts`. This function accepts the transmit power in watts (`ptW`), transmit antenna gain in dBi (`gtDb`), frequency in Hz (`fHz`), range in kilometers (`rangeKm`), receiver bandwidth in Hz (`bandwidthHz`), receiver noise figure in dB (`noiseFigureDb`), and system noise temperature in Kelvin (`tK`). It returns the SNR in dB by computing the free-space path loss via the `freeSpacePathLossDb` helper, converting transmit power to dBW, and subtracting the total noise floor expressed in dBW. The default parameters of this function—50 kW transmit power, 10 dBi transmit gain, 100 MHz frequency, 8 MHz bandwidth, and 4 dB noise figure—correspond to a typical FM broadcast illumination scenario and serve as the reference configuration for the worked examples in Section 9.4.

The Friis equation in this receive-only formulation is remarkably compact yet powerful. It immediately reveals the scaling behavior of the received signal: doubling the range reduces the received power by approximately 6 dB, a fact that has profound implications for coverage estimation and is explored in detail in Section 9.5. The equation also makes transparent the trade-off between frequency and range: higher frequencies suffer greater path loss for a fixed range, which is why FM broadcast (VHF band) penetrates buildings and covers large distances more effectively than UHF television or microwave satellite links, all else being equal.

## 9.2 The Noise Floor

Every received signal must be evaluated against the background noise floor. In any practical receiver, the minimum detectable signal is limited not by the sensitivity of the analog front-end alone but by the irreducible thermal noise generated by the physical temperature of the receiver components. This thermal noise, also called Johnson-Nyquist noise, arises from the random motion of charge carriers in resistive elements at any temperature above absolute zero. The power spectral density of this noise is flat (white) across the radio-frequency band of interest, and its total power within a given bandwidth is given by the fundamental thermodynamic relation:

$$N = k \cdot T \cdot \text{BW} \quad \text{[Watts]}$$

where $k = 1.380\,649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K is the Boltzmann constant, an exact SI-defined value that serves as a cornerstone of statistical mechanics. The temperature $T$ is the system noise temperature in Kelvin, and BW is the receiver bandwidth in Hertz. At the standard reference temperature of $T = 290$ K (approximately 17 degrees Celsius, close to typical room temperature), the thermal noise power in a 1 Hz bandwidth is approximately $-174$ dBm, a figure that every radio engineer memorizes as the fundamental noise floor benchmark.

Converting this noise power to the decibel-milliwatt scale yields the widely used engineering formula:

$$N_{\text{dBm}} = -174 + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(\text{BW}_{\text{Hz}}) + \text{NF} \quad \text{[dBm]}$$

In this expression, the $-174$ dBm/Hz term represents the thermal noise power spectral density at 290 K. The term $10 \cdot \log_{10}(\text{BW}_{\text{Hz}})$ adds the contribution of the receiver bandwidth: for an 8 MHz bandwidth, this contributes $10 \cdot \log_{10}(8 \times 10^6) = 10 \times 6.903 = 69.03$ dB, bringing the noise floor to $-174 + 69.03 = -104.97$ dBm before noise figure correction. The noise figure NF, expressed in dB, accounts for the degradation of the signal-to-noise ratio caused by the receiver's own electronic components—the low-noise amplifier, mixers, filters, and intermediate-frequency stages that comprise the receive chain. A typical consumer-grade receiver might have a noise figure of 3 to 6 dB, while a well-designed professional receiver might achieve 1 to 2 dB.

The noise floor is bandwidth-dependent: doubling the bandwidth raises the noise floor by 3 dB. This is a direct consequence of the additive nature of white noise across frequency. In the context of the worked examples, the FM broadcast signal bandwidth of 8 MHz and the DVB-T signal bandwidth of 8 MHz are treated as the relevant receiver bandwidths, and the Starlink-class link uses a wider 500 MHz allocation, which raises its noise floor by approximately $10 \cdot \log_{10}(500/8) \approx 8.9$ dB relative to the narrower-band FM case.

Figure 9.2 illustrates the noise floor as a function of bandwidth for three representative noise figures: 0 dB (theoretical ideal), 3 dB (typical consumer receiver), and 6 dB (budget receiver). The figure demonstrates that even an ideal noiseless receiver ($NF = 0$ dB) still faces a noise floor determined by thermal agitation, and that the noise figure adds a constant offset in dB across all bandwidths. The practical implication for civil-defense illumination assessment is that the receiver noise floor sets the absolute lower bound on detectable signal power, regardless of how sensitive the analog front-end is designed to be.

The `illuminatorSnrDb` function implements the noise floor computation internally via the expression `10 * Math.log10(K_BOLTZMANN * tK * bandwidthHz) + noiseFigureDb`, where `K_BOLTZMANN` is defined as the exact constant `1.380_649e-23` and `tK` defaults to 290. This produces the noise floor in dBW, which is then subtracted from the received power in dBW to yield the SNR. The choice of expressing both signal and noise in dBW ensures dimensional consistency and eliminates the need for repeated unit conversions that are a common source of error in manual link-budget calculations.

Understanding the noise floor is essential because it establishes the context in which the received signal must be judged. A received power of $-85$ dBm might sound impressively strong in absolute terms, but if the noise floor is at $-85$ dBm as well, the SNR is 0 dB and the signal is buried in the noise. Conversely, a received power of $-100$ dBm might seem weak, but if the noise floor is at $-130$ dBm, the SNR is 30 dB and the signal is clearly detectable. The SNR, not the absolute received power, is the metric that determines whether a signal is usable for information recovery.

## 9.3 SNR Definition and Labeling

The signal-to-noise ratio is the single most important metric in any communication link analysis. In the decibel domain, it is defined as the difference between the received signal power and the noise floor:

$$\text{SNR} = P_r(\text{dBm}) - N(\text{dBm})$$

Equivalently, using the Friis equation and the thermal noise expression, the SNR can be written in closed form as:

$$\text{SNR} = P_t + G_t + G_r - \text{FSPL} - (-174 + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(\text{BW}) + \text{NF})$$

All quantities are in dB or dBm, and the result is expressed in dB as a dimensionless ratio on a logarithmic scale. An SNR of 10 dB means the signal power is ten times the noise power; an SNR of 20 dB means the signal power is one hundred times the noise power; and so on. These thresholds have well-established engineering significance in the telecommunications literature.

An SNR greater than 10 dB is generally considered the minimum threshold for usable signal recovery in analog narrowband communication systems. At this level, the signal can be distinguished from the noise floor with adequate fidelity for voice or low-data-rate information. An SNR greater than 20 dB indicates excellent signal quality, suitable for high-fidelity analog demodulation or high-rate digital modulation schemes with robust forward error correction. In the context of civil-defense illumination assessment, an SNR above 10 dB at a given geographic location confirms that the illuminator provides sufficient signal strength for emergency broadcast reception at that location. An SNR above 20 dB confirms that the reception margin is large enough to tolerate moderate fading, interference, or hardware degradation without loss of service.

Every computed SNR value throughout this work and in the associated software interface must carry the label **"SANDBOX SNR LITERACY"**. This label serves multiple critical functions. First, it ensures traceability of all civil-defense metrics by unambiguously identifying the origin and purpose of each computed value. Second, it prevents misinterpretation of the SNR as a targeting metric, an interceptor sensitivity parameter, or any other classification that could imply offensive capability. Third, it acts as a compliance mechanism that embeds the educational and planning intent of the computation directly into the output, making it impossible for the metric to be silently repurposed without modifying the label. The label is displayed alongside every numerical result in the user interface, in every generated report, and in every exported data file produced by the Palladium Null system.

## 9.4 Worked Example 9.1 — FM Broadcast Illumination

This example considers a standard FM broadcast station operating at 100 MHz with a transmit power of 50 kilowatts, illuminating a location at a range of 50 kilometers. FM broadcast is the archetypal illuminator of opportunity because it is ubiquitous in urban and suburban environments, operates at a frequency that provides good propagation characteristics, and uses a well-defined channel bandwidth of 8 MHz. The computations below demonstrate the complete chain from transmit power to final SNR, with every intermediate step shown explicitly.

The first step is to determine the wavelength of the illuminator signal. At 100 MHz, the wavelength is:

$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{299\,792\,458}{100 \times 10^6} = 2.9979 \text{ m}$$

This wavelength is characteristic of the VHF band and is relevant for antenna design considerations, though for this analysis the antenna gains are taken as given parameters.

The second step is to compute the free-space path loss at 50 km range. Substituting into the FSPL expression:

$$\text{FSPL} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 50\,000}{2.9979}\right) = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(209\,440) = 20 \times 5.321 = 106.42 \text{ dB}$$

This 106.42 dB of path loss represents the reduction in power density from the transmitter to the receiver location. It is a large number, but it is entirely consistent with the physics of spherical wavefront spreading: at 50 km, the power from a 50 kW transmitter is spread over a sphere of approximately $3.14 \times 10^{10}$ square meters, yielding a power density of roughly $1.6 \times 10^{-6}$ W/m² at the receive location.

The third step is to compute the received power. With $P_t = 50$ kW, we first convert to dBW:

$$P_t(\text{dBW}) = 10 \cdot \log_{10}(50\,000) = 46.99 \approx 47 \text{ dBW}$$

Assuming isotropic antennas in both transmit and receive ($G_t = G_r = 0$ dBi), the received power is:

$$P_r = 47 + 0 + 0 - 106.42 = -59.42 \text{ dBW}$$

Converting to dBm (adding 30 dB because 1 W = 1000 mW):

$$P_r = -59.42 + 30 = -29.42 \text{ dBm}$$

This received power level is well within the sensitivity range of any practical FM receiver, which typically requires only $-90$ to $-100$ dBm for adequate demodulation.

The fourth step is to compute the noise floor. For an 8 MHz bandwidth with a noise figure of 4 dB:

$$N = -174 + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(8 \times 10^6) + 4 = -174 + 69.03 + 4 = -100.97 \text{ dBm}$$

The logarithmic bandwidth term contributes 69.03 dB because the 8 MHz bandwidth is $10^{6.903}$ times the reference 1 Hz bandwidth. The 4 dB noise figure adds to this baseline, reflecting the noise contribution of the receiver's front-end electronics.

The fifth and final step is to compute the SNR:

$$\text{SNR} = -29.42 - (-100.97) = 71.55 \text{ dB}$$

This result, labeled **SANDBOX SNR LITERACY: SNR = 71.55 dB**, indicates an excellent signal-to-noise ratio that far exceeds the 10 dB usability threshold and even the 20 dB excellent threshold. At 50 km from a 50 kW FM broadcast illuminator with isotropic antennas, the received signal is overwhelmingly stronger than the thermal noise floor. The 71.55 dB margin means that even with significant fading, interference, or additional noise figure degradation, the signal would remain clearly detectable.

It is worth noting that the result of 71.55 dB is unusually high because the assumption of isotropic antennas ($G_t = G_r = 0$ dBi) is conservative. Real FM broadcast antennas typically have gains of 3 to 6 dBi depending on the radiation pattern, and a practical receive antenna might also contribute gain. Incorporating realistic antenna gains would increase the SNR further. The isotropic assumption was deliberately chosen to provide a conservative lower bound on the expected SNR, which is appropriate for the safety-oriented civil-defense assessment context.

The `illuminatorSnrDb` function in `src/physics/index.ts` computes exactly this result when called with its default parameters: `ptW = 50_000`, `fHz = 100e6`, `rangeKm = 50`, `bandwidthHz = 8e6`, and `noiseFigureDb = 4`. The function internally performs the same arithmetic chain: computing the free-space path loss, converting transmit power to dBW, computing the noise floor in dBW, and subtracting to obtain the SNR. The default `gtDb = 10` parameter in the function signature differs from the 0 dBi isotropic assumption used in this worked example, reflecting the fact that the default configuration assumes a modest transmit antenna gain that would increase the computed SNR further.

## 9.5 Worked Example 9.2 — Range Doubling

The relationship between range and received power in free space follows a well-known scaling law that is one of the most practically important results in radio propagation engineering. Because the FSPL term in the Friis equation contains $20 \cdot \log_{10}(R)$, doubling the range increases the path loss by:

$$\Delta\text{FSPL} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(2) = 6.02 \text{ dB}$$

Since the noise floor is independent of range (it depends only on bandwidth, noise figure, and temperature), every doubling of range reduces the SNR by exactly 6.02 dB. This inverse-square-law consequence is a powerful tool for rapid link-budget estimation and for understanding the coverage envelope of an illuminator.

Starting from the baseline FM broadcast scenario of Example 9.1 at 50 km with an SNR of 71.55 dB, we can immediately compute the SNR at any other range by subtracting 6.02 dB per doubling. At 100 km (one doubling from 50 km):

$$\text{SNR}_{100} = 71.55 - 6.02 = 65.53 \text{ dB}$$

The received power at 100 km is $P_r = -29.42 - 6.02 = -35.44$ dBm, and the noise floor remains at $-100.97$ dBm. The resulting SNR of 65.53 dB is still deeply in the excellent regime, far above the 20 dB threshold for high-quality reception. This means that an FM broadcast illuminator at 50 kW provides excellent coverage well beyond 100 km in free-space conditions, which is consistent with the well-known long-range reception of FM broadcast stations by receivers located far beyond the nominal service area, particularly during favorable propagation conditions such as troposcheric ducting or ionospheric reflection at night.

At 200 km (two doublings from 50 km):

$$\text{SNR}_{200} = 71.55 - 2 \times 6.02 = 59.51 \text{ dB}$$

At 400 km (three doublings from 50 km):

$$\text{SNR}_{400} = 71.55 - 3 \times 6.02 = 53.49 \text{ dB}$$

Even at 400 km, the SNR remains at 53.49 dB, which is firmly in the excellent category. This extraordinary range performance is a direct consequence of the combination of high transmit power (50 kW), favorable VHF propagation, and a relatively narrow signal bandwidth (8 MHz) that keeps the noise floor low. The 400 km result illustrates why FM broadcast coverage maps routinely show service areas extending well beyond the optical horizon, particularly in flat terrain such as the Great Plains or desert regions where the radio horizon can extend significantly beyond the geometric horizon due to atmospheric refraction.

The practical consequence of the inverse-square-law behavior is that the received power density decreases as $1/R^2$, and the field strength decreases as $1/R$. This means that the signal at 400 km is $1/64$ of the signal at 50 km in linear power terms, yet because the noise floor is constant and the baseline SNR is so high, the signal remains easily detectable. This phenomenon is of particular importance in civil-defense planning: even locations far from an FM broadcast tower can reliably receive emergency broadcasts, provided that the terrain does not introduce additional attenuation beyond free-space losses.

It is important to note that the free-space model assumes no obstructions, no multipath, no atmospheric absorption, and no interference from other sources. In practice, terrain shielding, building blockage, and ground reflections can significantly degrade the received signal at distances beyond the optical horizon. However, the free-space model provides a useful upper bound on performance and serves as the reference against which real-world measurements are compared. The SANDBOX SNR LITERACY framework explicitly acknowledges these limitations while using the free-space model as the computational baseline for all worked examples.

The range-doubling rule also provides an intuitive framework for comparing different illuminator technologies. A higher-power illuminator or one operating at a lower frequency (with lower FSPL for a given range) will maintain usable SNR at greater distances before the cumulative 6 dB per doubling penalty drives the SNR below the 10 dB usability threshold. This comparative analysis is the basis for the multi-illuminator comparison presented in Section 9.4.

## 9.6 Worked Example 9.3 — DVB-T UHF Illumination

Digital Television (DVB-T) transmitters operate in the UHF band and serve as powerful illuminators of opportunity, particularly in urban environments where digital television infrastructure is dense. This example considers a DVB-T transmitter operating at 600 MHz with a transmit power of 100 kilowatts, illuminating a location at a range of 30 kilometers. The DVB-T signal bandwidth is 8 MHz, consistent with the standard 8 MHz channel allocation used in most digital television broadcasting systems worldwide. The receiver noise figure is assumed to be 6 dB, reflecting a somewhat more complex receive chain than the FM broadcast case due to the need for tuner selectivity and intermediate-frequency processing.

The wavelength at 600 MHz is:

$$\lambda = \frac{299\,792\,458}{600 \times 10^6} = 0.4997 \approx 0.5 \text{ m}$$

The half-meter wavelength is characteristic of the UHF band and is relevant for antenna design, where typical dipole lengths are on the order of a quarter-wave (approximately 12.5 cm). The shorter wavelength compared to FM broadcast also means that the free-space path loss is greater for a given range, since FSPL is proportional to $1/\lambda^2$.

Computing the free-space path loss at 30 km:

$$\text{FSPL} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 30\,000}{0.5}\right) = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(753\,982) = 20 \times 5.877 = 117.55 \text{ dB}$$

This path loss is 11.13 dB higher than the 106.42 dB computed for the FM broadcast case at 50 km, despite the shorter range. The reason is the significantly higher frequency: at 600 MHz, the wavelength is one-sixth of the FM broadcast wavelength, and the path loss scales as $(f_2/f_1)^2$ for fixed range, contributing an additional $20 \cdot \log_{10}(6) = 15.56$ dB of path loss. The shorter range partially compensates: reducing the range from 50 km to 30 km reduces the path loss by $20 \cdot \log_{10}(50/30) = 4.44$ dB. The net effect is an increase of approximately 11.13 dB in path loss compared to the FM case.

The transmit power of 100 kW converts to dBW as:

$$P_t(\text{dBW}) = 10 \cdot \log_{10}(100\,000) = 50 \text{ dBW}$$

Assuming isotropic antennas ($G_t = G_r = 0$ dBi), the received power is:

$$P_r = 50 + 0 + 0 - 117.55 = -67.55 \text{ dBW} = -37.55 \text{ dBm}$$

The noise floor for an 8 MHz bandwidth with a 6 dB noise figure is:

$$N = -174 + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(8 \times 10^6) + 6 = -174 + 69.03 + 6 = -98.97 \text{ dBm}$$

The final SNR is:

$$\text{SNR} = -37.55 - (-98.97) = 61.42 \text{ dB}$$

This result, labeled **SANDBOX SNR LITERACY**, indicates that the DVB-T illuminator at 100 kW and 600 MHz provides an excellent SNR of 61.42 dB at 30 km range. Despite the higher path loss at UHF frequencies compared to VHF, the doubled transmit power (100 kW versus 50 kW) partially compensates, and the resulting SNR remains well above the 20 dB excellent threshold. This confirms that DVB-T transmitters are effective illuminators of opportunity for civil-defense reception assessment, even at frequencies where free-space path loss is more severe than at FM broadcast frequencies.

The comparison between Example 9.1 and Example 9.3 illustrates an important engineering trade-off. The FM broadcast illuminator at 100 MHz achieves a higher SNR (71.55 dB) than the DVB-T illuminator at 600 MHz (61.42 dB) despite having half the transmit power and operating at a greater range, because the lower frequency translates to substantially lower path loss. This frequency-dependent path loss is a fundamental characteristic of electromagnetic propagation and must be carefully accounted for when comparing illuminators operating at different frequency bands. The SANDBOX SNR LITERACY framework requires that all comparisons between illuminators at different frequencies include the complete FSPL computation to ensure fair and accurate assessments.

The DVB-T case also highlights the role of the noise figure. The 6 dB noise figure in this example raises the noise floor by 2 dB compared to the 4 dB noise figure in the FM example, reducing the SNR by an equivalent amount. In practical terms, a receiver with a higher noise figure requires a stronger received signal to achieve the same SNR, which can be critical when assessing reception at the edge of an illuminator's coverage area.

## 9.7 Worked Example 9.4 — Starlink-Class Link Budget

This example considers a Starlink-class satellite downlink as an illuminator of opportunity, representing the highest-frequency and most technologically advanced scenario analyzed in this work. The Starlink satellite constellation operates in the Ku and Ka bands, with typical downlink frequencies around 12 GHz. The satellite transmits at approximately 250 watts with high-gain phased-array antennas that can achieve gains of 45 dBi or more in the beam direction toward Earth. This example analyzes a ground receiver located at a range of 500 km (approximately the altitude of a low Earth orbit satellite) with a large-dish receive antenna also having a gain of 45 dBi, operating over a 500 MHz bandwidth with a noise figure of 2 dB characteristic of a well-designed satellite receiver.

The wavelength at 12 GHz is:

$$\lambda = \frac{299\,792\,458}{12 \times 10^9} = 0.02498 \approx 0.025 \text{ m}$$

The 2.5-centimeter wavelength is in the microwave band and requires precision antenna engineering. A 45 dBi parabolic dish at this wavelength would have a physical diameter on the order of 1 to 1.5 meters, depending on the aperture efficiency and feed illumination pattern.

The free-space path loss at 500 km is computed as:

$$\text{FSPL} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 500\,000}{0.025}\right) = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(251\,327\,412) = 20 \times 8.400 = 168.00 \text{ dB}$$

This 168 dB of path loss is enormous compared to the FM broadcast and DVB-T examples, but it is partly compensated by the exceptionally high antenna gains of 45 dBi in both transmit and receive. The transmit power of 250 W converts to dBW as:

$$P_t(\text{dBW}) = 10 \cdot \log_{10}(250) = 23.98 \approx 24 \text{ dBW}$$

The received power, accounting for both antenna gains, is:

$$P_r = 24 + 45 + 45 - 168 = -54 \text{ dBW} = -24 \text{ dBm}$$

The noise floor for a 500 MHz bandwidth with a 2 dB noise figure is:

$$N = -174 + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(500 \times 10^6) + 2 = -174 + 87.00 + 2 = -85 \text{ dBm}$$

The $10 \cdot \log_{10}(500 \times 10^6) = 87.00$ dB contribution from the 500 MHz bandwidth is substantially larger than the 69.03 dB from the 8 MHz bandwidth used in the FM and DVB-T examples. This 18 dB increase in the noise floor due to the wider bandwidth is a significant penalty, but it is offset by the large antenna gains and the relatively low noise figure of the satellite receiver.

The final SNR is:

$$\text{SNR} = -24 - (-85) = 61 \text{ dB}$$

This result, carrying the **SANDBOX SNR LITERACY** label, is remarkably comparable to the DVB-T example's 61.42 dB despite the vastly different operating parameters. The Starlink-class link achieves an excellent SNR at 500 km range through the combination of high transmit power (250 W), extraordinary antenna gains (45 dBi each), and a low noise figure (2 dB), which collectively overcome the enormous 168 dB of free-space path loss at microwave frequencies.

The Starlink example serves several important pedagogical purposes within the SANDBOX SNR LITERACY framework. First, it demonstrates that the Friis equation applies universally across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, from VHF broadcast to microwave satellite links, with the same mathematical form and the same physical interpretation of each term. Second, it illustrates that high antenna gain can compensate for severe path loss, which is the fundamental principle behind the design of satellite communication systems and deep-space tracking networks. Third, it shows that the SNR metric provides a consistent basis for comparing vastly different system architectures, allowing the engineer to assess whether any given illuminator-receiver pair provides adequate signal quality for the intended application.

The comparison across all four examples reveals a consistent pattern: despite enormous differences in frequency, range, transmit power, and bandwidth, the SNR values cluster in the 53 to 72 dB range, all well above the excellent threshold of 20 dB. This is because each example represents a scenario where the illuminator is sufficiently powerful and the receiver is sufficiently sensitive that the signal dominates the noise by a large margin. The SANDBOX SNR LITERACY label ensures that these results are understood in the proper context: they are order-of-magnitude estimates for civil-defense reception assessment, not predictions of actual system performance under real-world conditions that include multipath fading, atmospheric attenuation, interference, and hardware imperfections.

## 9.9 Dual-Use Framing

The signal-to-noise ratio is a fundamental electromagnetic metric with broad applicability across the engineering spectrum. It is used in radar systems to assess target detectability, in electronic warfare to characterize jammer effectiveness and interceptor sensitivity, in telecommunications to evaluate link quality, and in scientific instrumentation to determine measurement sensitivity. The mathematical formalism of SNR computation is identical across all these domains; only the context, interpretation, and intended application differ.

The dual-use framing of SNR calculations requires careful consideration. The same computation that assesses whether a civil-defense receiver can pick up an FM broadcast signal at a given location could, in principle, be applied to assess whether a hostile receiver can intercept a military transmission or whether a jammer can overwhelm a friendly link. The Friis equation, the thermal noise floor, and the SNR definition are all domain-agnostic mathematical tools. The distinction between legitimate and illegitimate applications lies not in the mathematics but in the intent, the parameters chosen, and the context in which the results are presented.

Within the Palladium Null framework, all SNR computations are explicitly scoped to civil-defense reception assessment only. The worked examples in this chapter are chosen specifically to illustrate the reception of civilian broadcast signals: FM radio, digital television, and commercial satellite downlinks. No parameters are computed for jammer design, no interceptor sensitivity thresholds are analyzed, and no scenarios are constructed that could be interpreted as providing guidance for the development of electronic warfare systems or offensive capabilities.

The dual-use framing also extends to the choice of parameters. The transmit powers used in the worked examples (50 kW for FM, 100 kW for DVB-T, 250 W for satellite) are typical values for civilian broadcast infrastructure and are drawn from publicly available specification data. The frequencies (100 MHz, 600 MHz, 12 GHz) correspond to allocated civilian bands and are published in international frequency allocation tables. The ranges (50 km, 30 km, 500 km) correspond to plausible civil-defense assessment scenarios. None of these parameters would be of interest to someone designing an offensive electronic warfare system, which would use entirely different power levels, frequencies, and geometries.

All figures computed in this chapter and in the accompanying software are for passive receive illuminators only. The receive-only philosophy means that the analysis stops at the antenna input of the receiver and does not extend to demodulation, decoding, geolocation, or any other processing that might be applied to the received signal. The SNR metric is used solely to determine whether the signal is strong enough to be detected above the noise floor, not to assess what information might be extracted from the signal or what actions might be taken based on that information. This scoping discipline is a fundamental aspect of the civil-defense framework and ensures that the computational tools provided by Palladium Null remain within their intended domain of application.

The SANDBOX SNR LITERACY label reinforces this scoping by embedding the civil-defense context directly into every output. When an engineer or analyst sees the label, they are reminded that the computation is intended for reception assessment and that the results should not be extrapolated or repurposed for any other application. The label functions as a contextual guardrail that complements the parameter scoping and the documentation discipline that together constitute the dual-use governance framework of the Palladium Null project.
