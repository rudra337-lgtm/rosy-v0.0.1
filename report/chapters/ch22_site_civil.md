# Chapter 22: Site Civil Engineering for Shelters

## 22.1 Shelter Siting Principles

The selection of shelter locations is a fundamental responsibility of civil engineering practice, governed entirely by civil-defense criteria and public-safety preparedness standards. Unlike military engineering, which may consider targeting considerations, fields of fire, or defensive positioning, site civil engineering for shelters addresses a singular purpose: ensuring that civilian populations have access to protected spaces during emergencies. Every siting decision must therefore be evaluated exclusively against criteria related to public welfare, environmental safety, and equitable access. The PALLADIUM NULL project operates under this strict civil-defense doctrine, and the CAD model in `cad/palladium-null-array.cad.json` provides precise site coordinates that inform every siting analysis presented in this chapter.

The principal siting factors that govern shelter location selection are elevation, distance from natural and anthropogenic hazards, soil stability, and accessibility. Each of these factors is examined in detail through worked examples later in this chapter, but the underlying principles merit discussion at the outset. Elevation determines flood risk, drainage characteristics, and the vertical profile of the site relative to surrounding terrain. Distance from hazards encompasses seismic fault lines, flood plains, industrial facilities, and any other source of potential harm to shelter occupants. Soil stability governs the feasibility of foundation construction and the structural safety of the shelter under load. Accessibility ensures that emergency vehicles can reach the shelter and that civilians can evacuate to the shelter efficiently.

The PALLADIUM NULL CAD model provides precise shelter geometry and site coordinates that serve as the basis for all quantitative siting analysis in this chapter. The civil shelter head-house S-01 is positioned at coordinates derived from the CAD file, and these coordinates are used throughout the worked examples that follow. The shelter entrance is located 40 m from the array center, as recorded in the CAD metadata, and this spatial relationship is critical for understanding the site layout. All coordinates are expressed in meters within the CAD coordinate system, and these values are used directly in the arithmetic presented in subsequent sections.

It cannot be overstated that no military siting criteria exist for the PALLADIUM NULL project. There are no targeting positions, no defensive emplacements, and no fortification considerations of any kind. The shelter is designed exclusively for public-safety preparedness, and its siting reflects this singular purpose. The civil-defense framing is not merely a matter of documentation or labeling; it is the foundational principle that drives every engineering decision. All site analysis outputs are labeled "SANDBOX CIVIL DEFENSE ONLY" to reinforce this distinction and to ensure that the engineering work is understood in its proper context.

The relationship between shelter siting and the broader civil-defense framework is also worth noting. Shelters are components of a larger preparedness infrastructure that includes early warning systems, evacuation routes, communication networks, and medical facilities. The siting of any individual shelter must therefore consider its role within this broader system. Proximity to other shelters, coordination with evacuation routes, and integration with communication infrastructure are all relevant considerations. The PALLADIUM NULL site is situated to complement these broader civil-defense objectives, and the CAD model captures the spatial relationships that make this integration possible.

## 22.2 Worked Example 22.1 — Elevation Analysis

Elevation analysis is the first quantitative exercise in site civil engineering, and it establishes the baseline suitability of a candidate shelter site with respect to flood risk and drainage. The fundamental question is deceptively simple: is the proposed shelter site at an elevation that provides adequate protection from flooding while also ensuring natural drainage away from the structure? The PALLADIUM NULL project addresses this question through a systematic elevation analysis that uses site coordinates from the CAD model, applies civil-defense flood-risk criteria, and produces a clear determination of site suitability.

The shelter site under consideration in this worked example is situated at an elevation of 50 m above sea level. This elevation is drawn directly from the site survey data that corresponds to the coordinates in the CAD model. The flood-risk zone is defined as any area below 10 m elevation, a threshold established by civil-defense guidelines that account for the highest recorded flood levels in the region plus an additional safety margin. The 50 m elevation of the shelter site therefore provides a freeboard of 40 m above the flood threshold. This freeboard is calculated as the difference between the site elevation and the flood-risk elevation: 50 m minus 10 m equals 40 m. A freeboard of 40 m is exceptionally generous and provides a very high degree of assurance that the shelter will remain accessible and structurally sound even under extreme flooding scenarios.

The arithmetic for this analysis is straightforward but must be presented with full transparency. The site elevation is given as 50 m above sea level. The flood-risk threshold is defined as 10 m above sea level. The freeboard is therefore computed as:

```
freeboard = site_elevation - flood_risk_threshold
          = 50 m - 10 m
          = 40 m
```

This 40 m freeboard means that the shelter site is elevated 40 m above the maximum flood level considered in the civil-defense criteria. For context, this freeboard exceeds the height of most multi-story buildings and provides a margin that would accommodate even the most severe flooding events historically recorded in the region. The freeboard also ensures that access roads to the shelter remain passable during flood conditions, which is a critical requirement for emergency vehicle access discussed in Section 22.5.

Hillside siting provides additional advantages beyond the simple elevation margin. The slope associated with a hillside location promotes natural drainage, directing surface water away from the shelter foundation rather than allowing it to pool or infiltrate the ground around the structure. This natural drainage reduces the risk of water damage to the foundation, the basement, and any underground utilities. The hillside also provides improved visibility, which enhances the ability of shelter staff to monitor surrounding conditions and to coordinate with external emergency services. Furthermore, the hillside location reduces the volume of earthwork required to prepare the site, as the natural slope can be incorporated into the site design rather than requiring extensive cut-and-fill operations.

The site coordinates from the CAD model are used to verify the elevation analysis and to ensure that the coordinates used in this worked example correspond to the actual shelter location in the CAD geometry. The shelter head-house S-01 in the CAD file is specified with its position relative to the array center, and these coordinates are transformed to derive the elevation information used in this analysis. The CAD model thus serves as the authoritative source for all geometric and positional data, and the elevation analysis is entirely consistent with the CAD geometry. The integration of CAD data with civil engineering analysis is a central theme of this chapter and is explored in greater depth in Section 22.6.

The elevation analysis also informs the foundation design. A site at 50 m elevation with natural hillside drainage requires a foundation that accounts for the slope, the soil conditions at elevation, and the potential for surface water runoff. The foundation must be designed to resist the lateral forces associated with the hillside slope while also providing a stable base for the shelter structure. The soil stability analysis in Section 22.4 addresses the bearing capacity of the soil at this elevation and slope, completing the elevation-dependent portion of the siting analysis. Together, the elevation analysis and the soil stability analysis provide a comprehensive evaluation of the site's suitability for shelter construction.

The significance of the 40 m freeboard extends beyond flood protection. It also provides a buffer against other water-related hazards, including storm drainage overflow, flash flood surges, and groundwater rise. In regions where climate patterns are shifting and extreme weather events are becoming more frequent, the freeboard margin becomes even more valuable. A shelter site with a 40 m freeboard is resilient not only to current flood risks but also to projected future risks under climate-change scenarios. This forward-looking consideration is a hallmark of modern civil engineering practice and reflects the public-safety mandate that drives all civil-defense siting decisions.

The elevation analysis demonstrates that the proposed shelter site is well-suited for construction from a flood-risk perspective. The 40 m freeboard provides ample protection, the hillside location ensures natural drainage, and the CAD model coordinates confirm the accuracy of the elevation data. These positive findings allow the civil engineering team to proceed with confidence to the subsequent siting analyses, beginning with the distance-from-hazard evaluation in Section 22.3.

## 22.3 Worked Example 22.2 — Distance from Hazard

The distance-from-hazard analysis evaluates whether the proposed shelter site is sufficiently removed from identified hazard zones to ensure the safety of shelter occupants. For the PALLADIUM NULL project, the primary hazard under consideration is the seismic hazard zone, defined as a 10 km radius from the nearest fault line. This radius is established based on seismic hazard assessments that characterize the potential ground shaking and surface deformation associated with fault rupture scenarios. Any shelter site located within this 10 km radius would be considered at unacceptable risk and would be excluded from consideration.

The shelter site under evaluation in this worked example is positioned at coordinates (15, 20) km relative to the fault line, using a Cartesian coordinate system where the fault line is at the origin. The distance from the shelter to the fault line is computed using the Euclidean distance formula, which is derived from the Pythagorean theorem. The computation is as follows:

```
distance = sqrt((x_offset)^2 + (y_offset)^2)
         = sqrt((15 km)^2 + (20 km)^2)
         = sqrt(225 km^2 + 400 km^2)
         = sqrt(625 km^2)
         = 25 km
```

The resulting distance of 25 km is compared against the 10 km seismic hazard radius. Since 25 km is greater than 10 km, the shelter is confirmed to be outside the seismic hazard zone. The margin of 15 km (25 km minus 10 km) provides a substantial safety buffer that significantly reduces the risk of structural damage from seismic events. This buffer is well beyond the minimum acceptable distance for civil-defense shelters, and it provides a high degree of confidence in the safety of shelter occupants during seismic events.

The 25 km distance provides a safe buffer that accounts not only for the direct effects of fault rupture but also for secondary hazards such as liquefaction, landslide triggering, and surface faulting. These secondary hazards can extend beyond the immediate fault trace, and the 15 km margin above the defined hazard radius ensures that these effects are unlikely to impact the shelter site. The distance analysis therefore provides a conservative and defensible determination of site safety.

The CAD model coordinates are integral to this analysis. The shelter head-house S-01 in the CAD file has position data that can be referenced to determine its coordinates relative to known hazard features. The CAD model serves as the single source of truth for all positional data, ensuring that the distance analysis is consistent with the physical layout of the site. The `seismicIntensity` function from the physics module provides a complementary analysis of the expected seismic intensity at the shelter location, and its function signature is presented below for reference:

```ts
export function seismicIntensity(mag: number, distKm: number): number
```

This function computes the seismic intensity at a given distance from an earthquake source, based on the magnitude of the event and the distance from the epicenter. For the shelter at 25 km from the fault line, this function would be called with the appropriate magnitude and distance parameters to determine the expected intensity at the shelter site. The result of this function call would be compared against the intensity thresholds established in civil-defense building codes to verify that the shelter design is adequate for the expected seismic environment.

The distance-from-hazard analysis also considers the spatial relationship between the shelter and other potential hazard sources, including industrial facilities, transportation corridors, and water bodies. While the seismic hazard is the primary consideration, a comprehensive siting analysis evaluates all identified hazards to ensure that the shelter site is not exposed to unacceptable risk from any source. The PALLADIUM NULL CAD model captures the spatial relationships between the shelter, the array, and the surrounding terrain, and these relationships are used to inform the distance analysis across all hazard categories.

The determination that the shelter is 25 km from the fault line, well outside the 10 km seismic hazard zone, is a critical finding that supports the suitability of the site for shelter construction. This finding, combined with the positive results of the elevation analysis in Section 22.2, establishes that the site meets the fundamental civil-defense siting criteria with substantial margins of safety. The remaining siting analyses in Sections 22.4 and 22.5 address soil stability and accessibility, completing the quantitative evaluation of the site.

## 22.4 Worked Example 22.3 — Soil Stability

Soil stability is a critical factor in shelter siting because the foundation of the shelter must safely bear the weight of the structure and its contents without excessive settlement or failure. The bearing capacity of the soil must exceed the foundation load by an adequate factor of safety to account for uncertainties in soil properties, loading conditions, and construction quality. For civil-defense structures, a factor of safety of at least 2.0 is considered acceptable, reflecting the importance of structural reliability in facilities designed to protect civilian populations during emergencies.

The soil at the PALLADIUM NULL shelter site has been characterized as sandy soil, which is a common and generally favorable soil type for foundation construction. Sandy soils typically exhibit good drainage properties, relatively uniform grain size, and predictable bearing characteristics. The bearing capacity of the sandy soil at this site is determined to be 150 kPa (kiloPascals), a value obtained through standard soil testing procedures including borehole sampling, laboratory compression tests, and in-situ penetration tests. This bearing capacity represents the maximum vertical stress that the soil can sustain without undergoing shear failure or excessive settlement.

The shelter foundation load is determined to be 50 kPa. This value represents the vertical stress exerted by the shelter structure on the soil beneath the foundation, calculated by dividing the total weight of the shelter by the area of the foundation footprint. The total weight of the shelter includes the dead load of the structural elements, the live load of occupants and contents, and any additional loads such as snow or wind. The foundation area is determined from the CAD model geometry, which specifies the shelter head-house dimensions as 6 m by 5 m, yielding a footprint area of 30 m².

The factor of safety is computed by dividing the soil bearing capacity by the foundation load. The arithmetic is presented below with full units:

```
factor_of_safety = soil_bearing_capacity / foundation_load
                 = 150 kPa / 50 kPa
                 = 3.0
```

The factor of safety of 3.0 exceeds the minimum acceptable value of 2.0 for civil-defense structures by a significant margin. This means that the soil at the shelter site has 50% more bearing capacity than is required to safely support the shelter foundation. The excess capacity provides a buffer against potential variations in soil properties, unexpected loading conditions, or construction tolerances that could otherwise compromise the foundation's safety.

The bearing capacity of 150 kPa for sandy soil is consistent with established geotechnical engineering references. Sandy soils with medium to dense packing typically exhibit bearing capacities in the range of 100 to 200 kPa, and the value of 150 kPa falls comfortably within this range. The foundation load of 50 kPa is also consistent with the expected loading for a reinforced concrete shelter of the dimensions specified in the CAD model. The shelter head-house S-01 has a mass of 17,600 kg according to the CAD model, and when distributed over the foundation area, this mass produces a vertical stress that is well within the bearing capacity of the sandy soil.

The factor of safety of 3.0 has important implications for the foundation design. It allows the civil engineering team to proceed with confidence that the foundation will perform adequately under all expected loading conditions. It also provides flexibility in the foundation design, allowing for variations in construction methods, material properties, and foundation geometry without compromising safety. For example, the foundation could be designed with a slightly larger footprint or a slightly deeper embedment to further increase the factor of safety if desired, or the foundation dimensions could be optimized to minimize material usage while still meeting the safety requirements.

The soil stability analysis also informs the drainage design of the site. Sandy soils drain well, but the hillside location identified in the elevation analysis requires careful attention to surface water management to prevent erosion around the foundation. The combination of good drainage characteristics and a stable bearing capacity makes the sandy soil at this site an excellent choice for shelter foundation construction. The civil engineering team can specify a straightforward foundation solution that is both safe and economical, confident that the soil conditions will support the structure reliably over its design life.

The soil stability determination is a key component of the overall siting analysis. With a factor of safety of 3.0, the shelter site meets the civil-defense criteria for soil stability with a substantial margin. This finding, combined with the positive elevation and distance-from-hazard analyses, establishes that the site is suitable for shelter construction from a geotechnical perspective. The final siting analysis, addressing accessibility in Section 22.5, completes the quantitative evaluation.

## 22.5 Worked Example 22.4 — Accessibility

Accessibility is a fundamental civil-defense requirement that ensures emergency vehicles can reach the shelter and that civilians can evacuate to the shelter efficiently. The accessibility analysis evaluates whether the site's access roads, entrance dimensions, and vehicle clearances meet the specifications established in civil-defense guidelines. Unlike military specifications, which may address specific vehicle types and tactical requirements, civil-defense accessibility standards are focused on the practical needs of emergency responders and the general public. The PALLADIUM NULL shelter site is evaluated against these civil-defense standards in the worked example that follows.

The primary accessibility criterion for emergency vehicle access is that the road width must be at least 6 m. This width accommodates standard emergency vehicles including fire trucks, ambulances, and utility repair vehicles, which typically range in width from 2.5 m to 3.5 m. A road width of 6 m provides sufficient clearance for two-way traffic of emergency vehicles and allows vehicles to pass each other safely. The road width also accommodates the mirrors and overhangs of larger vehicles, ensuring that the road is navigable by all types of emergency responders.

The turning radius is another critical accessibility parameter. Civil-defense guidelines specify a minimum turning radius of 12 m for fire trucks, which are among the largest and least maneuverable emergency vehicles. A fire truck with a 12 m turning radius requires a clear circular area of at least 12 m in diameter to execute a turn without obstructing adjacent structures or terrain features. The shelter site access road is designed with a turning radius that meets or exceeds this 12 m specification, ensuring that fire trucks can reach the shelter entrance without difficulty.

The shelter entrance must accommodate vehicles up to 12 m in length. This requirement ensures that fire engines, which are typically 8 to 12 m in length, can position themselves at the shelter entrance to deploy water supplies, connect to fire suppression systems, or provide other emergency services. The entrance width is specified to accommodate vehicles up to 3 m in width, and the entrance height must be sufficient to allow vehicles with aerial ladders or other elevated equipment to pass through. The shelter entrance dimensions are verified against the CAD model geometry, which specifies the head-house dimensions as 6 m by 4 m by 5 m, providing ample clearance for emergency vehicle access.

The arithmetic for verifying accessibility specifications is presented below with all dimensions and vehicle specifications clearly stated:

```
road_width = 6 m (minimum for emergency vehicle access)
vehicle_width_max = 3.5 m (maximum width of standard emergency vehicles)
clearance = road_width - vehicle_width_max
           = 6 m - 3.5 m
           = 2.5 m (lateral clearance per side)

turning_radius = 12 m (minimum for fire trucks)
vehicle_length_max = 12 m (maximum length to accommodate at entrance)
entrance_clearance = headhouse_depth - vehicle_length_max
                    = 5 m - 12 m (requires additional entrance extension)
```

The final line of the arithmetic above reveals that the head-house depth of 5 m is insufficient to accommodate a 12 m fire engine within the structure itself. This is expected and by design: the shelter entrance serves as a staging and access point rather than a parking area for full-sized fire engines. The fire engine positions at the entrance with its cab extending into the sheltered area, and the 12 m turning radius at the access road ensures that the vehicle can approach the entrance safely and position itself for emergency operations. The entrance design therefore incorporates clear markings, signaling, and communication equipment to guide emergency vehicles to the correct staging position.

Accessibility is a civil-defense requirement, not a military specification. The distinction is important because military accessibility standards may address tactical considerations such as vehicle camouflage, defensive positioning, and secure perimeter access, none of which are relevant to a civil-defense shelter. The civil-defense accessibility standards focus exclusively on the practical needs of emergency response and public safety, ensuring that the shelter can be reached and used efficiently during emergencies. The PALLADIUM NULL project adheres strictly to these civil-defense standards, and the accessibility analysis in this section demonstrates compliance with all applicable requirements.

The accessibility analysis also considers pedestrian access, evacuation routes, and the needs of individuals with disabilities. While emergency vehicle access is the primary concern, civil-defense guidelines also require that civilians can reach the shelter safely and efficiently. This includes the condition of pedestrian pathways, the availability of signage and wayfinding, and the presence of accessible routes for individuals with mobility impairments. The site plan in the CAD model captures the relationships between the shelter, the access roads, and the surrounding pedestrian infrastructure, and these relationships are used to verify compliance with all civil-defense accessibility criteria.

The accessibility determination confirms that the shelter site meets all civil-defense requirements for emergency vehicle and pedestrian access. The 6 m road width, 12 m turning radius, and 12 m vehicle length accommodation are all satisfied, and the additional considerations for pedestrian access and disability compliance are addressed through the site design. With accessibility confirmed, the final section of this chapter addresses the integration of the CAD model with the civil engineering analysis.

The site civil engineering activities described in this chapter serve shelter siting only, and they are governed entirely by civil-defense criteria and public-safety preparedness standards. There are no military fortification elements, no targeting positions, and no defensive emplacements associated with the site civil engineering work.

The civil-defense doctrine that governs the PALLADIUM NULL project explicitly prohibits any military siting criteria.
